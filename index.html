I am completing my PhD in neuroscience on an NSF fellowship at Yale University in Dr. Todd Constable’s laboratory. My research focuses on using machine learning and fMRI functional connectivity data to identify where an individual’s disordered circuits are located. The overarching goal of my research is to leverage these approaches to inform a wide range of translational network neuroscience problems, including biomarker identification, subtyping heterogenous psychiatric disorders, treatment prediction and personalization, and mapping compensatory neural circuitry.

Previously, I was a data analyst at the Neuroscape Center at UCSF. There, I conducted clinical trials evaluating the efficacy of cognitive therapeutics in a variety of clinical populations.

Overview

My research lies at the intersection of neuroimaging analysis methods development and translational neuroscience. Specifically, I have built upon brain-behavior modeling techniques, like connectome-based predictive modeling (CPM), to better understand circuit dysfunction in mental illness. My recent work has focused on building tools that identify where an individual’s specific disordered circuits are that contributes most to their multivariate symptom profile. These disordered circuit models have helped us better understand how similar psychiatric symptoms can arise from different dysfunctional systems depending on factors like a patient’s diagnostic history. We have also recently shown that the extent to which disordered circuitry impinges upon cognitive systems is a strong predictor of whether a patient will experience cognitive impairment in conjunction with their symptomatology, which is a potentially meaningful factor for guiding precision treatments.

Currently, I am working on applying disordered circuit modeling in the following other translational and methodological contexts:

  1. Characterizing individualized mechanisms of responsiveness to interventions.
  2. Identifying compensatory mechanisms that protect against cognitive decline when cognitive systems are impaired.
  3. Developing biologically-informed disorder subtyping to guide precision medicine frameworks.
  4. Improving brain-behavior model generalizability by better accounting for interindividual heterogeneity in which circuits are related to symptomatology.
